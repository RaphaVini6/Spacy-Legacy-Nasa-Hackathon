## 1. The project in one minute

**Problem.** Since 1967, NASA has left dozens of landers, rovers, science stations, memorials and pieces of debris
on the Moon, on Mars and in deep space. Most people don't know they exist, and almost nobody thinks about them as
something that could be useful again. At the same time, robotics is still taught mostly with abstract examples,
and space content is rarely accessible to deaf or blind students.

**Solution.** Spacy Legacy is an educational web museum of that space heritage:

- an **exhibit** of 27 real artifacts, each with its mission, location, scientific goal and an idea of how its parts
  could be reused by future missions;
- a **mission simulator**: students pick a real mission, choose the hardware (power, battery, insulation, wheels,
  radio, computer) and watch day by day whether their probe survives the cold, the dust and the radiation;
- a **teaching section** that turns space recycling into a robotics project for the classroom;
- **accessibility from the start**: four languages, Libras (Brazilian Sign Language) through the government's
  VLibras, audio narration, high contrast, larger text and reduced motion.

**Why it matters.** Reusing 10 to 50 kg of metal, arms and motors that are already on the Moon or on Mars saves the
fuel and money needed to launch that mass from Earth. And there is a real precedent: in 1969 the Apollo 12 crew
walked to the Surveyor 3 lander and brought about 10 kg of its parts back to Earth.

Good examples to quote:

- **Opportunity** was planned for 90 sols and worked for more than 14 years, until the 2018 global dust storm.
  In the simulator, the real hardware survives 5,234 days - very close to the real 5,251. Swap the solar panels
  for a nuclear generator (MMRTG) and the probe survives the storm.
- **Ingenuity** was planned as a 30-day demonstration and lasted more than 1,000 days. In the simulator, its real
  hardware lasts 1,000 days, against 1,027 in reality.
- **Ingenuity** flew 72 times, when only 5 flights were planned.

## 3. Pitch script (3 minutes)

Adjust the speakers to the team. Times are cumulative.

**0:00 - 0:25 | Hook**
> "Right now, on the Moon and on Mars, there are dozens of pieces of NASA equipment that most people have never
> heard of. Landing stages, rovers, a helicopter, science stations that still produce heat. We call it space
> heritage - and we think it is also a resource."

**0:25 - 0:50 | Problem**
> "This heritage is scattered in technical archives, in English, and almost never reaches schools. Robotics is
> taught with abstract examples. And space content is rarely made for deaf or blind students."

**0:50 - 1:30 | Solution (show the site)**
> "Spacy Legacy is a web museum of this heritage. Each artifact shows its mission, where it is, what it did and
> how its parts could be reused. For example, the Apollo 15 lunar rover: its wheels and electric motors could
> become an autonomous cargo trailer, without sending a new vehicle from Earth."

**1:30 - 2:10 | Simulator (the "wow" moment)**
> "And students can test it themselves. Here is Opportunity, which worked for 14 years on solar power until a
> dust storm covered the Sun. Let's swap the solar panels for a nuclear generator... and launch."
> (show the countdown, the day counter and the result)
> "Now it survives the storm. In one minute, the student understood a real engineering trade-off."

**2:10 - 2:35 | Accessibility and education**
> "The whole site works in four languages, with Libras through the government's VLibras and audio narration for
> blind students. And there is a teaching section with a three-phase method to turn this into a robotics project:
> map the parts, plan the robotic disassembly, and calculate the payload savings."

**2:35 - 3:00 | Impact and close**
> "Reusing 10 to 50 kg already on the Moon saves the cost of launching that mass from Earth - and Apollo 12 already
> did it in 1969. Spacy Legacy turns that history into a classroom. Thank you."

Tips:

- Rehearse with a timer. If the slot is shorter, cut the education part first and keep the simulator.
- One person talks while another drives the demo.
- Look at the judges, not at the screen.

## 6. Live demo script

Order that tells the best story in about 2 minutes:

1. **Home** - let the animation play for a second; point to the numbers (27 artifacts, 13 missions).
2. **Exhibits** - click "Mars", then search "rover". Open **Lunar Roving Vehicle** (or Perseverance).
3. **Artifact page** - read the "How it could be reused" box; show the location map; click **Listen** (audio) and
   **Libras**.
4. **Simulator** - "Simulate this mission" on **Opportunity**; change Power to **MMRTG**; **Launch**.
   Comment while the days count up; show the score and the comparison with the real probe.
5. **Language** - switch to Portuguese or Spanish to show the content changes too.
6. **Learn** (if there is time) - the 3-phase method and the payload calculator.

Backup plan: if the internet or the computer fails, use screenshots or a recorded video of the same flow.
Libras needs internet (VLibras is an online service); narration works offline for audio that was already played once.

## 7. Project documentation

### What the team built

| Part | Technology | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Frontend | React + Vite | the website (museum, simulator, learning pages) |
| Backend | ASP.NET Core (.NET 10) | data, simulation engine, narration texts |
| Libras API | ASP.NET Core | Portuguese text -> Libras gloss using VLibras (gov.br) |
| Speech API | ASP.NET Core | text-to-speech for blind users |
| Docker | Docker Compose + nginx | runs everything with one command |

The site only talks to the backend; the backend talks to the two accessibility APIs. Everything runs with
`docker compose up -d --build` (site on port 8080). The code repository has the full technical README.

### Data and sources

- The 27 artifacts come from the team's research document, based on official NASA documents and the
  NASA Space Science Data Coordinated Archive (NSSDCA). Each artifact page links to its NSSDCA catalog entry.
- Photos are from the NASA Image and Video Library (public domain); the 3D renders were made by the team.
- **Honest note:** names, dates, functions and locations are facts. The ideas for physically reusing parts are
  conceptual engineering proposals for teaching, and should be verified independently for real projects.

### How the simulator works

Day by day, the simulator checks: energy produced (solar depends on light and dust, nuclear does not), battery
charge and degradation, day/night cycle (the lunar night lasts about 14 Earth days), cold and heat limits of the
parts, dust on the panels and wind cleaning, real dust storms (2007 and 2018), radiation on the computer, wear of
wheels and rotors, and whether the radio can reach Earth. When there is not enough power the probe hibernates;
when not even hibernation is possible, the mission ends.

Values are simplified on purpose: the goal is to teach trade-offs, not to replace engineering software. Even so,
with the real hardware the results stay close to history: Opportunity 5,234 simulated days vs 5,251 real,
Ingenuity 1,000 vs 1,027, Viking Lander 1 2,305 vs 2,305, Mars Pathfinder 75 vs 85.

### Accessibility

- **Deaf users:** the official VLibras widget signs the page text, and each artifact can be translated into a
  Libras gloss. VLibras only translates Portuguese, so the Portuguese text is always used.
- **Blind users:** every artifact and every simulation result can be narrated in the site's language, and the text
  is written to be heard (short sentences, dates spelled out). If the speech service is unavailable, the browser's
  own voice reads it.
- **Everyone:** high contrast, three text sizes, reduced motion, keyboard navigation and screen-reader labels.

### Teaching method (3 phases)

1. **Payload identification** - students list the motors, sensors, cameras and arms of inactive probes.
2. **Robotic disassembly and reconfiguration** - in simulation or with classroom kits, a service robot drives to
   the equipment, releases connectors and integrates the recovered part into itself.
3. **Payload savings** - students calculate how much it costs to launch the same mass from Earth.

Four hands-on cases: Viking Lander 1 (sampler arm), Apollo 15 rover (wheels and motors), InSight (1.8 m arm) and
Ingenuity (carbon fiber blades and motors). The robotics background follows Andrade, *Estudo Comparativo Aplicado ao
Planejamento e Controle de Trajetórias de Robôs Móveis* (UFC, 2011): signal delay and autonomy, non-holonomic
constraints and cascade control (GPC + PID).

### Next steps

- More artifacts (Soviet and other agencies' missions, more debris).
- Pilot with schools and teachers; activity sheets for each phase.
- Classroom mode in the simulator (challenges with a goal and a budget).
- Mobile version and offline mode for schools with poor internet.

## 8. Questions the judges may ask

**Is the simulator realistic?**
It is simplified on purpose, for teaching. But it uses real conditions (lunar night, Martian dust, the 2007 and
2018 storms) and with the original hardware it lands close to the real lifetimes - Opportunity 5,234 simulated
days vs 5,251 real ones.

**Where does the data come from?**
Official NASA sources and the NSSDCA catalog, linked on every artifact page. Reuse ideas are clearly marked as
conceptual.

**Is reusing hardware on the Moon actually possible?**
There is a precedent: Apollo 12 recovered about 10 kg of Surveyor 3 in 1969. NASA also reuses designs - InSight
was built on the Mars Phoenix platform, and the SkyFall concept builds on Ingenuity.

**How does the accessibility work?**
VLibras (from the Brazilian government) for Libras, a speech service for narration with a fallback to the
browser's voice, plus contrast, text size and reduced motion options.

**Who is it for?**
High school and university students, robotics teachers and science museums - and anyone curious about space.

**How would it grow?**
New artifacts are just data files, so the exhibit grows without code changes. Next: school pilots and a classroom
mode with challenges.

**Why these technologies?**
React for an interactive site, .NET for a robust API, separate services for the accessibility features so they can
be swapped or scaled, and Docker so anyone runs the whole project with one command.
